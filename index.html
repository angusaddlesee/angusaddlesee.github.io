About

I’m an Applied Scientist at Amazon Alexa+, working on LLM routing and optimisation — designing systems that select the right model for each interaction to balance accuracy, latency, and cost for millions of users. I moved from academia to industry to help build a product that reaches people at that scale, with a particular focus on making voice interactions more natural and accessible.

Before Amazon, I completed a PhD in Artificial Intelligence on conversational AI for people with dementia, and contributed to the EU-funded SPRING project, deploying a multi-party social robot in a hospital memory clinic. Earlier, I worked as a machine learning engineer on NLP projects with the NHS and Scottish Government, using information extraction to improve patient outcomes.

I co-founded the European and African Conversational AI Summits, a SIGdial officer, and have given 50+ talks at venues including Harvard, USC, KTH, BBC, and NatWest.

Featured Projects

SPRING Hospital Memory Clinic Robot

A multi-party conversational robot using LLMs, deployed in a real hospital memory clinic as part of the EU-funded SPRING project. Eight research institutes collaborated on the challenges of social robots in healthcare. My focus was the spoken dialogue system — specifically, enabling the robot to naturally converse with patients, companions, and clinicians simultaneously, rather than the one-on-one interaction today’s systems assume.

Voice Assistants for People with Dementia

My PhD research on accessible dialogue systems for people living with dementia. Today’s voice assistants are trained on data representing the “average” user and misunderstand speech patterns that differ from the norm. Mid-sentence pauses and anaphora usage, for example, become more common and pronounced with dementia. I collected real interactions with Alexa devices to identify exactly which changes cause failures, and designed tweaks to current systems that make them more accessible and more naturally interactive for everyone.

Links: 📰 Amazon Science article · 🔗 Project details

Voice Assistants for Visually Impaired People in the Kitchen

Supervised 30 MSc students building voice assistants that help visually impaired users read food labels, locate items using stationary objects as spatial anchor points, and make informed decisions about food safety and nutrition. Designed with explainable AI so users can ask follow-ups like “how sure are you about that?” — published across two papers.

selected publications

  1. Socially Pertinent Robots in Gerontological Healthcare
    Xavier Alameda-Pineda, Angus Addlesee, Daniel Hernández García, and 41 more authors
    In International Journal of Social Robotics, 2025
    Abs

    This paper examines whether autonomous social robots with conversational abilities can be practically useful in elderly care settings. We conducted two experimental phases at a Parisian day-care facility, testing a full-sized humanoid robot with roughly 60 end-users including patients and caregivers. We assessed acceptability using the Acceptability Evaluation Scale and usability via the System Usability Scale. Our findings reveal that users are receptive to this technology, especially when the robot’s perception and action skills are robust to environmental challenges. We also document user concerns and suggestions for improvement in this previously underexplored gerontological healthcare environment.

  2. Incremental multi-party conversational AI for people with dementia
    Angus Addlesee
    Heriot-Watt University, 2024
    Abs

    This thesis develops incremental multi-party conversational AI systems designed to support people with dementia. Current voice assistants assume turn-based interaction with a single user, but clinical settings involve multiple participants with varying cognitive abilities. We present systems that process speech incrementally, manage multi-party hospital conversations, and adapt to the speech production changes associated with dementia including extended pauses, interrupted utterances, and word-finding difficulties.

  3. You Have Interrupted Me Again!: Making Voice Assistants More Dementia-Friendly With Incremental Clarification
    Angus Addlesee and Arash Eshghi
    Frontiers in Dementia, 2024
    Abs

    Speakers seldom have a full plan of what they are going to say in advance and require incremental planning during speech, often resulting in pauses. This is particularly important for accessibility, as individuals with dementia experience longer and more frequent pauses, yet current voice assistants interrupt prematurely. We developed SLUICE-CR, a corpus containing 3,000 human-generated incremental clarification requests (iCRs), and tested large language models’ ability to both generate and respond to these requests appropriately. Our findings show that larger LLMs interpret incremental clarificational exchanges more effectively, indicating these models can be configured to handle speech patterns typical of people with dementia and reduce user frustration.

  4. Multi-party Multimodal Conversations Between Patients, Their Companions, and a Social Robot in a Hospital Memory Clinic
    Angus Addlesee, Neeraj Cherakara, Nivan Nelson, and 5 more authors
    In Proceedings of the 18th Conference of the European Chapter of the Association for Computational Linguistics (EACL), 2024
    EACL Best Demo Award Abs

    true

    We present an LLM-based spoken dialogue system deployed in a hospital setting, embodied by the ARI social robot. The system enables patients and companions to have conversations together with multimodal input and output including speech, video, and gestures. It determines when to participate in conversation, generates human-like clarification requests when patients pause mid-utterance, answers in-domain questions grounded in provided knowledge, and appropriately responds to out-of-domain requests such as jokes and quizzes. The system’s ability to handle unexpected patient utterances represents a significant advancement in real-world deployment of dialogue systems.

  5. Clarifying Completions: Evaluating How LLMs Respond to Incomplete Questions
    Angus Addlesee, Oliver Lemon, and Arash Eshghi
    In Proceedings of the Joint International Conference on Computational Linguistics, Language Resources and Evaluation (LREC-COLING), 2024
    Abs

    Humans process language incrementally, which leads to conversational phenomena like incremental clarification requests (iCRs) when someone pauses mid-sentence. We created SLUICE-CR, a corpus containing 3,000 human-generated iCRs, to evaluate whether large language models can generate appropriate clarifying responses to incomplete questions. We find that the ability to produce contextually suitable iCRs only appears in larger language models and requires prompting with example iCRs from our corpus. The results suggest that autoregressive language models can understand and generate language incrementally, with implications for making conversational AI systems more accessible for users with memory impairment.
