## Overview

My **PhD research** focused on designing conversational AI for people with dementia. Speech production changes as dementia progresses, but today's voice assistants are trained on huge datasets that work well for the "average" user but not for groups whose speech differs from the norm. This work was deployed in a hospital memory clinic via the SPRING project, and informs how I think about accessibility now that I work on the models behind Alexa+.

## Problem

Voice assistants like Alexa work very well for most users, but people with dementia experience speech production changes that cause frequent misunderstandings. Mid-sentence pauses become more common and more pronounced, interruptions increase, and word-finding difficulties emerge. These changes aren't accounted for in systems trained on "typical" speech patterns, making voice assistants less accessible for vulnerable populations who could benefit most from voice-controlled independence.

## Human-Centred Approach

This research was grounded throughout in collaboration with the people it was designed to support:

- **Working directly with people with dementia and their carers**: They were participants in studies, not just subjects. Their lived experience shaped the research questions and the interpretation of findings.
- **Healthcare professional collaboration**: Clinicians informed how the work would translate from research lab to memory clinic.
- **DEICTIC corpus**: Collected real interactions between people with dementia and Alexa devices to identify exactly which speech changes occur and which cause the voice assistant to misunderstand. This empirical grounding revealed specific patterns like extended mid-sentence pauses and incomplete utterances.

## Why This Helps Everyone

Improving conversational fluidity for people with dementia improves the experience for everyone. Mid-utterance pauses aren't unique to dementia: non-native speakers pause while translating a word, and we all hesitate when we momentarily forget a name or song title. **Designing voice assistants that handle these patterns gracefully makes them more accessible _and_ more natural for every user.**

## Real-World Deployment

The findings from this PhD didn't stay in the lab. They were integrated into a multi-party social robot deployed in a real hospital memory clinic via the EU-funded **[SPRING project](/projects/3_project/)**, where it interacted with patients, their companions, and clinicians. The research moved from understanding the problem, to designing solutions, to seeing them tested with the very people they were built for.

## From Memory Clinic to Alexa+

I now work in **Amazon Alexa's Frontier AI Modelling Lab** on the models behind **[Alexa+](/projects/1_project/)**. The lessons from PhD research inform how I think about routing, evaluation, and reward signals at scale: accessibility is not a separate workstream, it shapes good conversational AI for everyone. I bring up these points internally to advocate for accessibility-aware design wherever I can.

## Research Highlights

**Speech Production Changes**: Empirically identified specific speech changes in dementia that cause voice assistant failures, including:

- Extended mid-sentence pauses (often misinterpreted as end-of-utterance)
- Incomplete or interrupted questions
- Word-finding difficulties and self-corrections
- Slower speech rate and increased hesitations

**Talks**: I've spoken about this work at venues including Harvard, USC, KTH, BBC, and NatWest, alongside community talks (Pint of Science, Borrow a Researcher) bringing the research to broader audiences.
