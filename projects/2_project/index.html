Voice Assistants for People with Dementia

PhD research on accessible dialogue systems tailored to cognitive abilities and communication patterns of dementia patients.

Overview

My PhD research focused on designing conversational AI for people with dementia. Speech production changes as dementia progresses, but today’s voice assistants are trained on huge datasets that work well for the “average” user but not for groups whose speech differs from the norm. This work has informed industry best practices for inclusive voice AI and continues to influence accessibility-focused design at Amazon Alexa.

Problem

Voice assistants like Alexa work very well for most users, but people with dementia experience speech production changes that cause frequent misunderstandings. Mid-sentence pauses become more common and more pronounced, interruptions increase, and word-finding difficulties emerge. These changes aren’t accounted for in systems trained on “typical” speech patterns, making voice assistants less accessible for vulnerable populations who could benefit most from voice-controlled independence.

Approach

Data Collection & Analysis: Collected real interactions between people with dementia and Alexa devices to identify exactly which speech changes occur and which changes cause the voice assistant to misunderstand. This empirical approach revealed specific patterns like extended mid-sentence pauses and incomplete utterances.

Accessibility-Focused Design: Focused on how to tweak current voice assistants to make them more accessible for people with dementia, and more naturally interactive in general. Developed recommendations for handling interrupted questions, extended pauses, and other speech variations.

Clinical Collaboration: Worked with healthcare professionals and people with lived experience of dementia to ensure research was grounded in real needs and practical constraints.

Key Contributions

This research demonstrated the critical importance of designing AI systems that consider cognitive diversity and accessibility from the ground up, rather than as an afterthought. The work has influenced both academic research and industry practice, showing that voice assistants can be made more accessible through careful attention to speech variation patterns and dialogue repair strategies.

Research Highlights

Speech Production Changes: Identified specific speech changes in dementia that cause voice assistant failures, including:

  • Extended mid-sentence pauses (often interpreted as end-of-utterance)
  • Incomplete or interrupted questions
  • Word-finding difficulties and self-corrections
  • Slower speech rate and increased hesitations

Design Recommendations: Developed practical guidelines for improving voice assistant accessibility:

  • Longer timeout thresholds for end-of-utterance detection
  • Better handling of interrupted and incomplete questions
  • More patient and supportive error recovery strategies
  • Clearer feedback about what the system understood

Broader Impact: This work contributes to making voice technology more inclusive and accessible for all users, not just those with dementia. Many of the accessibility improvements benefit older adults, people with speech differences, and anyone in challenging acoustic environments.
